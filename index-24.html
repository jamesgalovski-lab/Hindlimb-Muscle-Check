Hindlimb Muscle Check

A hands-on way to feel your dog's thigh and calf muscles, record what you find, and track it over months. Use it to decide when a change is worth a conversation with your veterinarian.

Educational tool, not a diagnosis. This guide teaches a hands-on check and keeps your records. It does not examine your dog or tell you what is wrong. If you are worried, see your veterinarian.

Why check the hind legs

The hind legs drive your dog forward and lift them up from lying down, onto the couch, and up the stairs. When muscle there shrinks, those everyday movements get harder. Muscle loss in the legs can come from aging, from using a sore leg less, from joint, back or nerve problems, or from long-term illness such as heart or kidney disease.

Muscle loss often happens slowly. A monthly hands-on check, done the same way each time, makes small changes easier to notice.

Muscle is not the same as fat

A body condition score (BCS) estimates fat. It does not measure muscle. A dog can carry extra fat and still be losing muscle underneath. In a 2026 CT study of 39 dogs, dogs with a higher BCS had a smaller share of muscle in their hind-leg cross-sections, and older dogs did too. That is why this guide asks for your dog's BCS and why the two scores are most useful side by side.

By feel, healthy muscle is firm and rounded and springs back slightly. Fat is softer and moves over the surface. Bone feels hard and close to the skin.

The scale this guide uses

Each of three spots on each hind leg gets a score from 0 to 3. The two thigh spots plus the calf give a leg total out of 9.

3Normal. Rounded, firm muscle. You can't feel the bone through it. 2Mild loss. Slightly flatter or thinner. Still firm. 1Moderate loss. Clearly less muscle. You can feel the bone. 0Severe loss. Very little muscle. The bone is easy to feel.

What this tool can and can't do

The scale is adapted, in plain language, from the hindlimb muscle condition score published by Yun and colleagues in 2026. In that study two experienced veterinarians scored the dogs and agreed closely, and their scores matched CT measurements of the muscles. The study included mostly small breeds, and few dogs had moderate or severe loss.

The scale has not been tested with owners scoring their own dogs at home. Your scores are your observations. They are most useful for spotting change in your own dog over time, especially when the same person does every check.

The guidance about when to call your vet is a starting framework written for this tool. It is not a research-derived cut-off.

When to see your vet

Call promptlyYour dog shows pain when touched, or there is sudden weakness, dragging a leg, or not putting weight on a leg.
Book a visitAny spot scores 1 or 0; one leg is clearly smaller than the other; scores keep falling; or muscle drops while weight or BCS stays the same or rises.
Mention itAny spot scores 2, a 1-point drop since last time, or new mobility changes. Raise it at your next routine visit.
Keep trackingEvery spot scores 3 and nothing has changed. Check again in about a month.

You know your dog. If something worries you, call your vet whatever the score says.

Do a check

About five minutes. Do both hind legs, one spot at a time.

Before you start
  • Have your dog stand relaxed and square on a non-slip floor. A helper can hold their head or offer a treat.
  • Use the same person, the same spots and roughly the same time of day for every check.
  • Use gentle, steady pressure. You are feeling shape and firmness, not pressing hard.
  • Compare left with right as you go.
  • Stop if your dog flinches, yelps, licks their lips, or pulls away. Tick the discomfort box below and call your vet.
Optional: mid-thigh tape measure

Measure around the thigh halfway between the hip joint and the knee, dog standing square, tape snug but not tight. Use the same soft tape each time. This number includes fur and fat as well as muscle, so treat it as a backup to the hands-on scores.
